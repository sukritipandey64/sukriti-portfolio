i The insight

The barrier isn’t information. It’s asking.

Specialty menus assume you already speak coffee: estates, processes, tasting notes. Beginners feel judged asking, so they order the hazelnut latte, or guess.

I still ask the barista what they’d recommend. Tasting notes confuse me too. You can love blueberries and hate blueberry coffee.

ii The idea

Two picks, in plain words.

Scan the QR on the table, say what you usually drink, get a safe pick and a stretch pick from today’s menu. A two-tap check-in after the cup makes the next one better.

The menu says

Attikan Estate, washed, V60 blueberry · floral · bright

In your words

Light and juicy. More iced tea than cold coffee.

Lo-fi wireframes. Grayscale first; the design system arrives when the research says what it needs.

iii Decisions so far

Small rules, strongly brewed.

  1. a

    No prompt needed

    Scan, one tap, two picks. Good help shouldn’t require knowing what to ask.

  2. b

    One question

    “What do you usually order?” It starts from what people already know.

  3. c

    Always two picks

    Safe and stretch. A miss is part of the experiment, not a failure.

  4. d

    Notes, translated

    Into food memories: jamun, jaggery, filter coffee.

  5. e

    A two-tap check-in

    The tone: come back and we’ll explore together.

  6. f

    Why not a chatbot?

    Picks come from this café’s menu, today. No general chatbot has that.

iv Early signals Signals to check, not findings

What a café co-founder in Chennai hears at the counter.

  • The decision gets handed over
    “A lot of them just end up asking ‘what do you recommend?’”

    So the app gives the recommendation people would otherwise ask a stranger for.

  • People start from the familiar
    “Most people look for something familiar first, like a cappuccino or a cold coffee.”

    So the first question stays “what do you usually drink?”

  • The words that trip people
    “Some don’t know what a specific processing is, like a washed, natural or honey… or a thermal shock.”

    So the glossary covers processing, not just flavours.

  • Curiosity is invited, never pushed
    “I don’t force anyone to get into specialty coffee… Once they ask a question, we go on explaining.”

    So the stretch pick reads as an invitation, and the safe pick is always there.

The conversations have started. Each point is a signal to test against the next ones.

v What’s next

Research first, this time.

My last project taught me to validate before I build. So before anything grows, the flow gets tested in a café with five or six beginners.

What I’ll watch for (hypotheses, not results)

  • A first-timer orders without asking “what do you recommend?”
  • Some choose the stretch pick, and the check-in comes back “just right”.
  • Baristas notice better questions at the counter.
