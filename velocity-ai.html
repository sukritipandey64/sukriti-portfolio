01 — The problem

Four projects. A teammate had one. Nobody could see it.

Velocity started as a conversation with Anant Mishra about how companies could measure the return on their AI spend. You can't measure that without knowing where time goes, and that led me to a problem I was living with every week.

I was running four projects while a teammate had one, and nobody could see it. Work was assigned on gut feel.

From there, I shaped the idea into a product, designed it, ran the interviews and built the prototype.

Project tracking asks

What work exists?

Answered well by task tools and spreadsheets.
Capacity intelligence asks

Can we realistically take this on?

Answered by nobody. It lives in a lead's head.

Design goalMove the capacity conversation to before a commitment is made.

04 — AI trust and control

AI assists. A human decides.

Velocity's AI plans projects and recommends people. Every output is designed to be checked by a person before it changes anything. Five patterns carry that principle.

a

Impact before Apply.

Each suggestion reads what → why → impact → decide. Review opens an impact panel: people and projects affected, hours moved, the effect on the timeline, a risk level. Apply lives only inside that panel. There is no way to act on a suggestion without seeing what it does.

b

Confidence on every AI number.

Scores never appear alone: Feasibility 87 · Model confidence 91%. The confidence sits beside the number as a quiet footnote, not a headline.

c

Leads set when the AI speaks up.

Flag anything under 70% confidence, warn below a health score of 60, treat a seven-day slip as risk. A team tunes the system to its own tolerance instead of accepting mine.

d

It refuses thin input.

Under 50 characters, Plan My Project says "Need more data to run this request" and asks for a requirements document instead. A confident plan built on too little is worse than no plan.

e

It admits it doesn't know yet.

An earlier version showed sample insights about named people on an empty workspace. On a real team, invented insights would cost trust on day one. Now the panel just says so.

And no spinner. While it works, the AI says what it's weighing:

  1. Analyzing similar projects…
  2. Mapping task structure…
  3. Simulating capacity constraints…
  4. Evaluating feasibility…

The AI recommends. The lead selects. Commit Project stays blocked until someone is chosen.

06 — Key decisions

Four decisions, and what each cost.

DecisionWhyTrade-off accepted
Plan before you commitCapacity is most useful before work is promised, not after a deadline slips.Planning becomes an extra step before execution.
Roles belong to teamsOne person can lead one team and contribute on another.The switcher became a team switcher, not a simple role toggle.
Hours and utilisation, not task countsFifteen small tasks can be lighter than two large ones.Every task needs an effort estimate.
No monitoringNo activity tracking, payroll or attendance. It must not feel like surveillance.Less data for the AI to learn from.

Deliberately not built yetAn "I'm interested" signal, so people can flag projects they'd like to join. Valuable, and it only helps once the capacity view is trusted. Adding it early would have widened the scope without strengthening the core loop.

08 — Reflections

What I'd tell myself in February.

01

Validate the edges, not just the core

I designed the core experience before validating it. The interviews didn't change that core, but they exposed what I'd missed: how the data gets in. That became the onboarding redesign.

02

Data needs a counterweight

Availability data alone can box people in. Showing it makes allocation faster, and it can also keep sending the same people the same work.

03

Directing AI is still design

I didn't draw these screens by hand. I wrote the briefs, judged the output and pushed it through six rounds. The decisions lived in the briefs.

What's next

  • Learn from rejections. Every ignored suggestion carries a reason. The next suggestion should use it.
  • An "I'm interested" signal, so allocation reflects growth as well as free hours.
  • Show how fresh progress data is, so a dashboard can't claim 90% done when it's 50%.
  • Move to semantic tokens, so a theme change is one edit, not a patch.
