Velocity is a personal project. It has no users and no shipped metrics. What it has is real research, real decisions, and a hypothesis that didn't survive contact with the people I interviewed. That's the case study.

01 — Context

Everyone thinks
they have capacity.

Engineering managers plan in spreadsheets that are stale before they're saved. They ask "who's free?" in chat, then discover their best engineer has been at 140% for a month. Jira tracks tasks, not people. The real model lives in the manager's head, and it breaks somewhere past fifteen people.

Starting hypothesis

Teams struggle with capacity visibility. An AI planning tool can make the invisible legible.

I designed the product first and tested the hypothesis second. In hindsight, that order is the most instructive thing here.

02 — The prototype

Two views. One truth.

A manager portal for planning and approvals, an employee portal for workload and time — with a view switcher for everyone who is both.

03 — Key design moments

Four decisions
worth defending.

01

Approving leave shouldn't be a guess.

Every interview described the same failure: risk shows up too late. So before a manager approves time off, Velocity shows what it costs — projects affected, hours lost, which critical-path tasks are exposed — and three ways forward, each with a confidence score.

Trade-off — an extra step on every approval. The next version should skip it entirely when the impact is zero.

02

The AI drafts. The manager commits.

"It's not as simple as counting tasks," one director told me. Judgement stays human. Plans live as editable drafts, separate from real projects, until a manager explicitly commits them — and every recommendation shows its reasoning and confidence rather than acting on its own.

Trade-off — a two-step wizard is slower than one click. Being wrong quietly is worse than being slow.

03

Empty states that do the job.

"No team members yet — go to Settings" is a dead end. Velocity's empty states carry the action inline: import a CSV, paste rows from a spreadsheet, or add people by hand — with skills suggested from each role, so setup doesn't become a data-entry chore.

Trade-off — far more design per screen. It's where the product starts to feel considered.

04

Directing the tool, not being directed by it.

Figma Make's first outputs were competent and generic. The work was in the correction: holding a point of view, rejecting the plausible-but-average, and writing constraints tight enough to get this interface rather than an interface.

The failure mode I learned to watch for: AI wires beautiful UI to invented data. An early dashboard proudly reported a named engineer at 120% — connected to nothing. Today the insights panel stays empty until real data exists. If the data looks too clean, it's fake.

One thing I got wrong in the system: a contrast fix that passed WCAG AA in the light theme quietly failed when the product went dark — 4.7:1 became 3.8:1. Same token, different background.

Type & components

Inter throughout; light 300 weights for page titles, 11px uppercase labels for structure. A small, reusable set — KPI card, status badge, and an AI suggestion card that always shows confidence, category and an Ignore option. The tool never gets the last word.

The fix that didn't survive

In the original light theme I swapped muted text from #A8A29E (2.5:1) to #78716C (4.7:1) to pass WCAG AA. Then the product went dark, and the same token on #141414 drops to 3.8:1 — below AA again. The lesson: a contrast fix is a fix for one background, not a property of the colour. It's first on the list for the next pass.

04 — What users told me

The research that
dismantled the premise.

Three semi-structured interviews, 30–60 minutes each, with people who staff and deliver work for a living. Small sample, labelled as such.

P1Audit director, Big FourRan resource deployment across engagements worth thousands of hours.
P2Product manager, B2B SaaSOwns roadmap prioritisation; works daily with engineering managers.
P3UX director, consultancyBalances staffing and utilisation against shifting client commitments.

Where they told me I was wrong

"The question today is not how many developers I have. The question is: what should those developers build?"

P2 — Product manager

"The first thing people would ask is, am I signing up to make my life more complex?"

P1 — Audit director

P2 was blunt: "I'm not the ICP for that." For product teams the scarce resource isn't hours, it's prioritisation. And P1 named the adoption killer — every insight Velocity produces depends on data someone has to keep feeding it. I had designed for the value of the output and ignored the cost of the input.

What held up

"I thought 90% of the work was done. When I actually talked to the person, it was only 50% done."

The problem, restated

Teams struggle with capacity visibility.

Teams struggle to predict delivery outcomes before problems become visible.

A small shift with a large consequence. The first framing builds a dashboard; the second builds an early-warning system — and dashboards were exactly what P1 kept pushing back on.

05 — Reflections

What I'd do differently.

01

Research before pixels

I designed 26 screens, then validated the problem. Reversed, I'd have built fewer screens and a sharper product. The most expensive thing I made was confidence in an untested premise.

02

Design for the input

The next version pulls signals from the tools teams already use instead of asking them to feed a new one — and leads with early warnings, not charts.

03

Talk to the real buyer

Three interviews, one persona each — directional, not validated. P2 pointed me at programme managers more than once; they're who I'd interview next.

"The strongest thing in this project isn't the interface. It's the interview where someone told me the premise was wrong — and that I kept it in."
