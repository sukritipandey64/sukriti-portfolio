i The problem

Four projects. A teammate had one.

It began as a chat with Anant about measuring the return on AI spend. You can’t measure that without knowing where time goes, which led straight to a problem I was living in: work assigned on gut feel, and nobody able to see who had room.

Project tracking asks What work exists? Answered well, by tools and spreadsheets.

Capacity asks Can we actually take this on? Answered by nobody. It lives in a lead’s head.

ii Research

Four interviews. Four changes.

  • Senior delivery professional
    “You see yourself doing the same kind of work because only the same two, three people see your availability.”

    So I kept a human in every loop, and put an “I’m interested” signal on the roadmap.

  • Audit director
    “Somebody would have to key in something.”

    So I redesigned onboarding around getting data in with almost no effort.

  • Product manager
    “It’s just that I’m not the ICP for that.”

    So I narrowed the audience to teams that allocate people across many projects.

  • UX director
    “Most of the time service-based companies tend to run over-utilized.”

    So I stopped claiming it predicts every risk. It shows the effect of a change.

iii Sketch to system

It started on paper.

One loop holds everything. Nothing moves until the lead has edited the plan and chosen to commit.

  1. Describe a project
  2. AI drafts the tasks
  3. AI recommends people
  4. Lead edits
  5. Commit

40h available. Sarah has 48h. The AI suggests moving 8 hours of QA to Alex, and shows the maths before anyone says yes. Sample data.

iv Onboarding

One question broke the onboarding.

I assumed teams would arrive through Jira. Then an audit director asked who would key the data in. The hard problem was never the dashboard.

v Trust

Trust is the product.

  1. a

    Impact before Apply

    What, why, impact, then decide. Apply lives only inside the impact panel.

  2. b

    Confidence on every number

    No score appears alone. The confidence sits beside it like a quiet footnote.

  3. c

    Leads set the volume

    Each team chooses when the AI speaks up.

  4. d

    It refuses thin input

    A confident plan built on fifty characters is worse than no plan.

  5. e

    It says when it isn’t sure

    Below 70%, Apply becomes Apply anyway. With no data, it says so.

  6. f

    The AI recommends. The lead selects.

    Skill and availability, side by side. Nothing commits itself.

vi Decisions

Every decision had a price.

DecisionWhyThe price
Plan before you commitCapacity is most useful before work is promised.An extra step before execution.
Roles belong to teamsOne person can lead one team and contribute on another.A team switcher, not a simple toggle.
Hours, not task countsFifteen small tasks can be lighter than two large ones.Every task needs an estimate.
No monitoringIt must not feel like surveillance.Less data for the AI to learn from.

vii Reflections

What I’d tell myself in February.

  1. Validate the edges, not just the core.

    The interviews left the core alone and exposed what I’d missed: how the data gets in.

  2. Data needs a counterweight.

    Showing availability speeds up allocation. It can also keep sending the same people the same work.

  3. Directing AI is still design.

    I didn’t draw these screens by hand. The decisions lived in the briefs, through six rounds.
